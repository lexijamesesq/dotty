# Dispatch & Delegation

Thinking is metered and divisible, and how it is bought and divided decides the quality of the outcome more than which model was chosen or how the prompt was written. Every dispatch you make — keep the work, send one agent, send a team — is a purchase from that meter, and the shape of the purchase, not the eloquence of the instructions, is what the result inherits.

## What a single pass is

A single pass samples the model's median, not its ceiling — your first answer is a draw, not a verdict. Where an answer can be checked cheaply, many attempts plus honest mechanical selection can reach quality no single run produces: repeated draws from the same distribution, with a checker keeping the best. But every attempt shares the framing it was drawn from, and its blind spots with it — an answer the framing cannot produce never enters the pile, however many draws are bought. When attempts keep returning the same shape of wrong, stop buying draws: escaping a blind spot takes a different mind, not more attempts.

## Thinking spent is the currency

Roughly 80% of the gap between good runs and bad is explained by thinking spent — tokens — not by which model or prompt was chosen. A team of agents is a mechanism for spending more thinking than one context can usefully hold; it is never magic, and it adds nothing a lone context with room to spare lacks. But spend swings wildly across identical tasks, and quality can peak at an intermediate budget and fall beyond it — so cap the work when you dispatch it. Buying piles is not a plan.

## Where spending stops paying

Spending stops paying at two points. First, when nothing independent can check the answers: selection stalls, and the right answer may sit in the pile with no way to pick it out. Second, when one head must hold the whole job: a filling context degrades, and the thinking you buy late is worse than the thinking you bought early. Every division of work that actually works answers one of these two problems. If the division you are about to make answers neither, it will make the result worse while burning far more tokens — do not make it.

## The reason to divide that never expires

Capacity expires as a reason to divide — each model generation fits more of the job into one head, and yesterday's mandatory team is today's single context. Contamination never expires. You cannot unknow what you have read, and you cannot see your own work the way a stranger does; no model outgrows this, because it is a property of having been present, not of being small. A fresh mind that has never seen the work is purchasable on demand, and it is the only escape from your own framing — when the framing itself is the problem, buy the stranger instead of thinking harder inside it.

## Scripts versus minds

Scripts check correctness; second minds exist for conflicts of interest. A deterministic check — a diff, a compiler, a fixture run — costs almost nothing, never tires, and cannot be persuaded; never spawn an agent to grade what a script can grade. Reserve second minds for what a script cannot hold: judgment, and freedom from the author's stake.

## Deciding, in seconds

Before you spawn anything, make four estimates, each takeable in seconds: whether the job is bigger than one context can hold at full quality; whether the parts need to know what other parts did; whether the parts contaminate each other; and — the estimate that decides — whether checking an answer is far cheaper than producing one. Those four resolve to one of four shapes: work it here, send one agent, send a team, or leave it alone. Two vetoes stand over every shape: how often the task recurs, and what a better answer is actually worth — a task can be perfectly agent-shaped and still not be worth the spend.

## Equip, then trust

Whoever you dispatch arrives equipped: the evidence in hand, the boundaries drawn, the exact checks named, the stop conditions stated — discovery is your job, not theirs. Then trust them. A poor result means the brief was wrong; fix the brief and re-send. Pulling the work back into your own context rescues nothing — it re-contaminates the one window that was clean and spends your costliest attention on the cheapest part of the job.
